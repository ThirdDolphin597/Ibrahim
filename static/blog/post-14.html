I am working on a ski bike and a carabiner as two separate projects, and Asiya, Jayden Wu, and I are turning the ski bike into our science fair project. The ski bike is a larger mechanical challenge because it has to combine the familiar shape and riding position of a bicycle with skis designed to travel over snow. The carabiner is a smaller and more compact project, but it is a useful opportunity to practice material selection, geometry, part layout, and manufacturing one strong, simple piece.

The ski bike will start with a bicycle frame purchased from an online marketplace. I need to get the frame before I can design the rest of the bike because I have to measure the geometry, spacing, and mounting points from the real part rather than guessing from a general bicycle model. Once I have the frame, I will be able to build the rest of the design around its actual dimensions and create mounting parts that fit it accurately.

The goal is to keep the frame as a normal bicycle frame and make the finished bike ride like a normal bike. The handlebars, pedals, seat, and existing frame layout will stay at the center of the design, while skis will replace the need for wheels on snow. I am keeping the project focused on this basic bike layout instead of adding complicated steering or control systems, so the result should feel recognizable and remain straightforward to operate.

I am building custom front and rear shocks for the ski bike so the suspension can be designed around the actual frame and weight distribution. The shock mounting geometry and travel are important parts of the science fair project because they will help determine how the bike responds to bumps and different snow conditions. I may also add sensors to measure how the shocks move in different environments, giving us data that can be compared across rides instead of relying only on observation.

The carabiner is a compact piece of metal with a carbide tip for scratching or scribing surfaces. It does not use springs or moving mechanisms; instead, the design relies on the natural tension and deflection of the cut metal. I have already completed the testing and am now getting ready to cut the final shape on the waterjet, likely today or next week. The goal is to create a rigid utility carabiner from one precisely cut part, with the geometry retaining enough tension to hold the shape while still allowing the piece to be practical for everyday use.
